# Thirty game concepts — working draft

Rank is design judgment, not playtest evidence. All are proposals, not approved builds. Each assumes a small foreground cast, deterministic input/replay, game-owned rules and a visible consequence or honest refusal. The first five are cases for consideration; estimates are relative and exclude existing HF migration and release work. These ideas do not imply that 4.x migration, performance gates or player acceptance are complete. The later request for concepts supersedes the older roadmap instruction not to draft new concepts; it does not itself authorize construction. New-game concept reviews and existing playtests remain separate obligations.

**Production default:** Unless an entry says otherwise, use Canvas 2D with simple geometric characters, flat-color shapes, reusable backgrounds and basic frame or transform animation. A solo developer can produce these with procedural shapes and openly licensed, attribution-compatible assets; no dedicated artist is assumed. The art is a proposal, not existing artwork. The named casts below are proposed characters, not verified members of the existing registry; check the cast registry and existing game continuity before adopting or replacing any name.

**Prototype test for the shortlist:** Show a complete repeatable loop, not just the opening. A person pursues a competing commitment, changes the scene, and forces a new player decision; an earlier interaction changes their later behavior. Compare the same situation with a fixed-response companion. In real time, make person decisions at deterministic fixed simulation ticks while motion continues; revalidate the action at execution and visibly cancel or renegotiate it if its preconditions changed. Test this with recorded inputs and replay. These are proposed design rules, not verified HF performance.

## 1. The Last Crossing — action / rescue

**Setting and art.** Willow Ford; indigo water, cream paper banks, cut-paper figures. Build with simple shapes and reusable textures.

**Proposed cast.** Mina, ferryman Orhan, and passengers Esra and Cem.

**Pitch.** Pilot a small ferry across a flooding river for one evening. Three people need to cross, but the boat, current and bank change each trip. You steer and handle the rope; passengers decide whether to board and what they will carry. An older ferryman can help but has a prior promise to return to his family.

**First 30 seconds.** The boat is already drifting toward a pier. Move the tiller to line it up, catch a thrown rope, and see the first passenger step toward the gangway. A rising water mark makes the immediate problem legible.

**Why a person matters.** Each passenger has a destination, fear, memory of a previous trip and a reason to refuse the current offer. If you leave someone behind to save a load of medicine, that person remembers the choice and may refuse your next request. The ferryman's help is negotiated, not a unit assigned to a slot.

**Causal example.** If Orhan remembers that Mina kept his earlier promise and is not already committed to leave, he may agree to one more trip; his boarding and later return make the result visible. This is a proposed interaction, not a demonstrated engine result.

**Stakes and failure.** Deliver the people and essential supplies before the last safe crossing. Grounding damages the boat; missed departures leave people on the wrong bank. People can refuse unsafe boarding. A partial rescue is an ending, not an invisible score penalty.

**Renderer/art.** Side-on Canvas 2D, large readable silhouettes, moving current and hand-drawn banks. Keyboard/controller steering with touch drag and accessible tap-to-steer. Water and passenger positions communicate state; dialogue stays brief and contextual.

**Size/risk.** Medium. One river, three landings, three passengers, one boat and a single evening. Risks: steering feel and accessible touch input; whether refusal remains understandable during motion; replay capture for fixed-step physics. Prototype the landing and one boarding refusal before adding a second crossing. HF should decide the person's response, while the host owns the boat and collisions.

## 2. The Repair Shift — workshop / direct manipulation

**Setting and art.** Copper Lane Repair; warm ochre bench, line-drawn tools and paper labels. Build with simple shapes and reusable textures.

**Proposed cast.** repairer Nila, colleague Faruk, and lamp owner Aylin.

**Pitch.** Run a neighborhood repair bench through a busy afternoon. Open, inspect and repair tangible objects while their owners wait, ask questions or leave. The player chooses what to tell them and whether to call a colleague for help.

**First 30 seconds.** Rotate a broken lamp, unplug it, remove two screws and expose a loose wire. The owner says she needs it tonight. One visual fault and two tools are immediately available.

**Why a person matters.** Owners have different deadlines, means and reasons for keeping an object. A colleague may accept a request, refuse because of a prior commitment, or teach a method after seeing your work. An honest admission of damage affects a later interaction; the repair itself remains player skill.

**Causal example.** If Faruk is tired and already promised to leave, he may refuse an urgent repair; the player must finish it alone or tell Aylin the deadline will be missed. This is a proposed interaction, not a demonstrated engine result.

**Stakes and failure.** Finish enough meaningful repairs to keep the bench open without breaking a promise. Incorrect repair wastes parts or breaks an object; rushing or deception can cost trust. A customer may leave and return later rather than always waiting.

**Renderer/art.** Close-up 2D cutaway objects, large draggable tools, illustrated waiting area. Tap alternatives to drag and no fine motor precision requirement. Objects physically change as work proceeds.

**Size/risk.** Medium. Three object types, three owners, one colleague and one afternoon. Risk: believable physical affordances become expensive content; constrain each object to a few visible operations and avoid a generic crafting menu. Test that different actions change the object and that the customer notices.

## 3. The Signal Crew — tactical coordination / stealth

**Setting and art.** North Bend railway; charcoal track, red signal light and rain streaks. Build with simple shapes and reusable textures.

**Proposed cast.** rail workers Selin, Murat, and Ipek.

**Pitch.** Guide a three-person crew along a storm-damaged railway to light a signal before a train arrives. Directly move the crew leader and ask the autonomous crew members to carry, hold or investigate. The route is a sequence of physical obstacles rather than a map of buttons.

**First 30 seconds.** Move to a fallen beam, lift one end, then ask another crew member to hold it. They agree or explain why they cannot; the gap visibly opens long enough to crawl through.

**Why a person matters.** The crew has remembered injuries, trust, different skills and commitments. A tired member may refuse a dangerous lift but suggest a safer route. Prior assistance affects willingness without guaranteeing obedience. Crew members retain their own control; the player cannot switch into someone to override a refusal.

**Causal example.** If Murat remembers Selin helping him and has enough capacity, he may hold the beam; if already committed elsewhere, he can refuse and explain before the team tries another route. This is a proposed interaction, not a demonstrated engine result.

**Stakes and failure.** Light the signal before the train reaches the damaged section. Losing time, dropping a load or stranding a crew member changes the available route. Failure ends in a missed signal and its visible consequences; do not simulate a disaster as a score multiplier.

**Renderer/art.** Top-down 2D tileless scene with broad paths, lights and weather; visible hands-on obstacles. Pointer, keyboard and gamepad; pause menu, slower speed and clear controls.

**Size/risk.** Medium–large. One short route, three obstacles, three people, one clock. Risk: companion navigation and deterministic replay. Keep pathfinding bounded and test one obstacle where the same request may be accepted or refused with a legible reason.

## 4. Corner Shop After Dark — shopkeeping / social simulation

**Setting and art.** Juniper Corner; amber candlelight, block-print shelves and deep blue shadows. Build with simple shapes and reusable textures.

**Proposed cast.** shopkeeper Rana, assistant Deniz, and customers Sami and Feride.

**Pitch.** Physically run a small shop during a power outage: place candles, carry stock, weigh goods and serve neighbors. A limited supply means an early sale can leave a later customer without what they need.

**First 30 seconds.** The lights go out; pick up a candle, place it beside the counter and hand a waiting customer a loaf. A second customer enters with a request that competes for the last loaf.

**Why a person matters.** Neighbors remember offers and broken promises, have different urgency and can reveal information or withhold it. An assistant can independently restock or refuse to stay late. Prices and inventory stay in the host, while the people decide how to respond.

**Causal example.** If Deniz remembers a broken promise about closing time and must collect a child, he may decline a late shift; the player sees him leave and must serve the remaining customer. This is a proposed interaction, not a demonstrated engine result.

**Stakes and failure.** Keep the shop operating until power returns and honor the promises you make. Spoilage, missed needs and people leaving are concrete outcomes; reaching closing time does not erase the effects of a bad allocation.

**Renderer/art.** Warm side-on shop cross-section with visible shelves and manipulable goods. Large tap targets and drag-to-counter, with tap alternatives; express stock by objects, not a spreadsheet.

**Size/risk.** Medium. One room, five recurring customers, one assistant, four products. Risks: a queue-management loop could overwhelm the social decisions; cap simultaneous visitors at two and make each interaction change stock or relationships.

## 5. The Ridge Run — driving / expedition

**Setting and art.** Slate Ridge; muted terracotta cliffs, layered paper mountains and a bright yellow truck. Build with simple shapes and reusable textures.

**Proposed cast.** driver Bora and passengers Leyla and Yusuf.

**Pitch.** Drive a battered delivery truck across one mountain route, stopping to clear hazards, load supplies and decide which people travel with you. Your passengers have reasons to push on or turn back; they are not vehicle upgrades.

**First 30 seconds.** Steer around a fallen branch, stop beside a stranded traveler, and choose where to put a spare tire so both the traveler and the load can fit. The truck visibly leans under an unbalanced load.

**Why a person matters.** A passenger may navigate, insist on a stop, or refuse an unsafe crossing based on knowledge and commitments. The player's earlier choice to listen or abandon supplies affects what they later offer. HF decides their actions; the host owns road physics and cargo.

**Causal example.** If Leyla knows the bridge is unsafe and trusts Bora enough to speak, she may urge a detour; Bora can ignore her, with the route and later response showing the result. This is a proposed interaction, not a demonstrated engine result.

**Stakes and failure.** Reach the clinic before its supply window closes with the needed medicine and as many people safe as possible. A damaged axle, bad load or delay can prevent arrival; sacrificing the medicine to help someone leads to a distinct ending, not an automatic game over.

**Renderer/art.** Side-scrolling 2D landscape with simple tactile suspension and large silhouettes. Responsive keyboard/controller input plus touch controls and optional slower driving. State is visible in cargo, road and vehicle behavior.

**Size/risk.** Large compared with the first four. One route, three stops, two passengers and one vehicle. Risks: driving feel and physical determinism may consume the project; prototype one bend, one stop and a meaningful passenger decision before expanding.

**Why this order.** The Last Crossing ranks first for immediate physical control and a compact, visible choice about people and cargo. The Repair Shift ranks second for a tactile loop with a small content footprint, but needs several crafted objects. The Signal Crew ranks third for strong cooperation, tempered by navigation complexity. Corner Shop ranks fourth because its social choices are clear, though queue pressure risks crowding them out. The Ridge Run ranks fifth because driving and travel are appealing but vehicle feel and deterministic physics carry the highest production risk of these five. This is a proposal, not an owner or player verdict.

## 6–30. Further candidates

Each entry gives the opening, HF contribution, stakes, visual approach, and relative size/risk in that order.

6. **The Kiln Floor — factory / direct control.** Setting: Red Clay Works; art: rust-red machines, cream tiles and stamped arrows, made from reusable shapes. Proposed cast: Dara and coworker Kenan. Start a belt, pick up a jammed clay tile and guide it into a kiln; arrange belts and firing stations to fulfill changing orders. A coworker may stop an unsafe line or teach a repair after being supported, changing throughput through their own decisions. Meet the shipment deadline without ruining the batch or losing the worker's help. Top-down illustrated machines show each tile moving and changing. **Medium–large:** conveyor logic and individual agency must both matter; prototype one jam and one refusal.
7. **The Orchard Year — farming / family.** Setting: Pear Hollow; art: soft green fields, seasonal patchwork plots and stitched borders, made from reusable shapes. Proposed cast: siblings Pinar and Eren. Walk into a small field, plant a seed and water a visibly dry row; crop rotations, weather and shared tools govern a season. A sibling may tend a crop, honor another promise or dispute a sale; bring in enough harvest to keep the farm while preserving or changing that relationship. Top-down seasonal fields and direct tool use. **Medium–large:** growing time and content can become repetitive; test one consequential week first.
8. **The Bell Ensemble — rhythm / performance.** Setting: Bell House; art: cobalt stage, gold circles and animated paper hands, made from reusable shapes. Proposed cast: musicians Ada, Tamer and Lale. Strike two visible notes in time with an on-screen pulse, then choose when to give another musician the lead. Each musician interprets missed cues and can accept, improvise or refuse a demanding rehearsal; finish a public performance with the ensemble still willing to play together. Animated hands, readable note paths and visual beat cues. **Medium:** timing accessibility and audio latency; support calibration, visual rhythm and slower practice.
9. **The Breakwater — tower defense / crew.** Setting: Saltmarsh Point; art: teal water, white contour lines and orange barriers, made from reusable shapes. Proposed cast: operators Cihan and Nermin. Place a movable flood barrier and manually close a gate as the first wave arrives; between waves reposition pumps and choose which approach to protect. Operators decide whether to staff an exposed pump, request relief or honor an earlier promise to another district. Prevent water reaching a shelter through three waves; a breach leaves a visible damaged district. Top-down coast, rising water and physical defenses. **Medium–large:** avoid encoding people as permanent tower bonuses; fixed-step wave and path simulation must replay.
10. **The Courtyard Circuit — arena action / team.** Setting: Sun Court; art: high-contrast coral court and bold circle-headed players, made from reusable shapes. Proposed cast: players Arda, Bilge and Veli. Move, dodge and pass a ball through a contested goal in the first thirty seconds; arena walls rotate between short rounds. Teammates choose when to support, improvise or decline a risky play based on prior cooperation and goals. Win a small tournament while keeping enough teammates willing and able to play; losing a round changes the bracket. Top-down 2D court with bold silhouettes and controller or keyboard input. **Large:** movement feel and teammate AI need a playable one-round prototype.
11. **The Archive Expedition — roguelike deckbuilder / exploration.** Setting: The Silt Archive; art: sepia rooms, inked cards and torn-paper edges, made from reusable shapes. Proposed cast: explorer Ilhan and companion Meryem. Play a rope card to cross a gap and a light card to reveal the next room; each expedition builds a small deck of equipment and shared plans. A companion independently accepts, alters or refuses a proposed card action according to knowledge and commitments; their help can add a temporary tactic, never guarantee obedience. Recover a document and escape before supplies run out; a failed run returns without it and changes the next offer. Illustrated rooms, cards that animate physical actions, visible companions. **Large:** legible consent and deck balance; bound the first build to one route and 12 cards.
12. **The Relay Vault — roguelite shooter / rescue.** Setting: Lantern Vault; art: black rooms, luminous cyan projectiles and stark silhouettes, made from reusable shapes. Proposed cast: rescuer Sevgi and survivors Baris and Zeliha. Move, aim and fire to clear a doorway, then carry a trapped person toward an exit; each seeded run rearranges rooms and offers temporary equipment. A rescued companion may cover the retreat, seek another person or refuse a dangerous route based on their own priorities. Extract people before the alarm seals exits; a failed run loses that attempt, while earned equipment unlocks new options rather than automatic victory. Top-down 2D rooms, readable projectiles and large character silhouettes. **Large:** shooting feel, procedural fairness and replayable input timing; prototype one room and one autonomous rescue decision.
13. **The Night Shift Manager — management / staffing.** Setting: South Dock; art: blue-gray floor, colored crates and painted lane lines, made from reusable shapes. Proposed cast: manager Suna and workers Tolga and Asli. Drag a proposed assignment onto a visible loading bay as a truck arrives; the worker decides whether to accept, then move a pallet yourself to unblock it; assign tasks, negotiate breaks and reorder deliveries across a shift. Workers have commitments and may accept, counteroffer or leave, and remember whether the player honored relief promises. Clear enough orders by closing without unsafe overload; missed shipments and departures visibly constrain the next day. Isometric loading floor with moving goods and people. **Medium–large:** meaningful autonomy must not become constant schedule repair.
14. **The Shifting Rooms — spatial puzzle / trust.** Setting: House of Hinges; art: ivory rooms, primary-color doors and geometric shadows, made from reusable shapes. Proposed cast: puzzle companions Yaren and Hakan. Push a block onto a floor switch to open a door, then rotate a room to join two corridors. A companion can hold a switch, suggest a route or leave an agreed post if a more urgent commitment arises; their refusal must be explained and the puzzle remain solvable or clearly fail. Get both people out before the building closes; a missed exit changes the ending. Clean top-down geometric rooms with visible moving walls. **Medium:** avoid a companion that is merely a puzzle object; author a handful of readable alternatives.
15. **The Block Party — party / social minigames.** Setting: Maple Court; art: bright bunting, marker-drawn props and confetti shapes, made from reusable shapes. Proposed cast: neighbors Dilan, Riza and Naz. Toss a beanbag at a moving target, then hand the next turn to one of three neighbors; alternate short local single-player events such as a relay, drawing and a shared balancing game. Neighbors can accept a team invitation, make a playful challenge or decline after a broken promise; the player cannot dictate their choices. Complete the gathering before rain, with event outcomes and relationships shaping the final photo. Bright illustrated courtyard and large readable controls, with timing alternatives. **Medium–large:** several minigames can multiply scope; begin with two and make it fun for one player without online play.
16. **The Lost Dog — tracking / search.** Setting: Fern Park; art: sage paths, paw-print stamps and leaf silhouettes, made from reusable shapes. Proposed cast: searchers Ahu and Kerem. Top-down park with visual traces and sound captions. **Medium:** evidence must be truthful and search never become pixel hunting.

Passengers have commitments, needs and reasons to wait or leave; complete a short route without abandoning someone or missing a critical connection. ## Existing-game visual rebuilds worth comparing before any new game

1. **The Pass:** physical kitchen, visible preparation and plating, direct movement and a consequential handoff; retain the existing four-week/rota design rather than inventing another campaign. The historical F-276/F-284 weakness was that actions did not visibly change enough; validate the current build before treating those findings as unfixed.
2. **The Night Watch:** a spatial watch floor where the player drags or taps a replacement onto a post, sees who takes over and hears an actual acceptance or refusal. Historical F-309 requested relief; verify current game state first.
3. **The Glance:** rebuild the instrument panel as a tangible, direct-control station; preserve its imperfect read and avoid exposing exact hidden states. Verify the current legal-action control and state before claiming a defect.
4. **The Warren:** a camera that follows an expanding playable space, with direct selection of visible people and places. A larger view must preserve legibility and consequential actions, not merely render more people.
5. **The Stairwell:** a navigable building with residents visible at doors and one bounded hidden story thread; retain source and truthful memory for claims. Do not turn the whole game into a relationship-map exercise.
